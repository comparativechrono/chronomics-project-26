3. Frequencies by continental group

A table of continental group against units; then, row by row, the fraction of five-repeat alleles (prop.table(tab, 1): each row adds up to 1).

tab <- table(d$superpopulation, d$repeats)
tab
freq5 <- prop.table(tab, 1)[, "5"]
round(sort(freq5), 3)
Check

The five-repeat allele: AMR 0.182, EAS 0.225, SAS 0.306, EUR 0.367, AFR 0.371.

How sure can you be of a frequency counted in 60 chromosomes (EUR)? If you had sampled 30 other Europeans, you would have got a somewhat different number. The standard error of a frequency p counted in n chromosomes is √(p(1 − p)/n), and about 95% of the time the true frequency lies within 1.96 standard errors of the estimate: the 95% confidence interval.

n <- rowSums(tab)
se <- sqrt(freq5 * (1 - freq5) / n)
round(cbind(n, freq5, lower = freq5 - 1.96 * se, upper = freq5 + 1.96 * se), 3)

4. Is the difference real?

The frequencies differ – but with so few chromosomes, could that be chance? The chi-squared test asks how likely counts this uneven would be if the frequency were the same in every group.

chisq.test(tab)
Check

X-squared = 13.3 with 4 degrees of freedom, P = 0.0099.

Think
  • What does P = 0.0099 mean, in words, and what does it not mean?
  • Look at the confidence intervals: which groups clearly differ, and which could well have the same frequency?
  • These are not random samples of their continents. How were the HPRC's people chosen (Liao et al. 2023)? Would you trust these numbers as "the frequency in Europe"? Stage 8 gives you 2,504 people to check them against.

6. Hardy–Weinberg

If people choose their partners regardless of their PER3 repeat, and nothing else interferes, the genotypes follow from the allele frequency p of the five-repeat allele: (1 − p)² are 4/4, 2p(1 − p) are 4/5 and p² are 5/5 – the Hardy–Weinberg proportions. Departures can mean selection, mixing of populations – or errors in the genotypes. Compare what you see with what you would expect:

g <- tapply(d$repeats, d$sample, function(x) paste(sort(x), collapse = "/"))
obs <- table(factor(g, levels = c("4/4", "4/5", "5/5")))
p5 <- mean(d$repeats == 5)
expected <- sum(obs) * c((1 - p5)^2, 2 * p5 * (1 - p5), p5^2)
rbind(observed = obs, expected = round(expected, 1))
x2 <- sum((obs - expected)^2 / expected)
c(chi2 = x2, p = pchisq(x2, df = 1, lower.tail = FALSE))
Check

Observed 113, 101 and 17; expected 115.7, 95.6 and 19.7. χ² = 0.75, P = 0.39: no sign of a departure.

Think

Here five groups with different frequencies were pooled. Pooling groups that differ makes too few heterozygotes (the Wahlund effect). Is that what you see? Why might it not show here? (Try the test within AFR alone.)
