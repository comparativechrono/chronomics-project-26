Stage 2 · Find the repeat

Week 2about 4 hours

Exon 19 of PER3 holds the repeat. Find it as if nobody had told you it was there: look for words that come back, measure the distance between them, line the copies up under each other – and translate them into the protein they make.

You will learn
  • to save what a command prints in a file (>), and to build a long command from short ones, one pipe at a time
  • to count every word of a sequence – its k-mers – and to find a word with grep
  • a little of regular expressions, the language of patterns in text
  • the genetic code and reading frames: from DNA to protein, by hand

1. The exon, in a file

In stage 1 samtools faidx printed exon 19 on the screen. > sends what a command prints into a file instead. (Careful: > replaces the file if it exists; >> adds to its end.) Then make a second file with the sequence alone, on one line: grep -v keeps the lines that do not match (here, all but the name), and tr -d "\n" deletes the ends of the lines.

cd ~/project
samtools faidx /data/examples/per3/reference/GRCh38_chr1_8Mb.fa.gz chr1:7829834-7830161 > results/exon19.fa
cat results/exon19.fa
grep -v ">" results/exon19.fa | tr -d "\n" > results/exon19.txt
wc -c results/exon19.txt
Check

wc -c counts characters: 328, the length of the exon. The sequence starts CAGTGTGTTACAGG and ends CAGCATCAG.

Read the sequence in exon19.fa for a minute. Can you see anything that comes back?

2. Words that come back

A tandem repeat is a word – a stretch of letters – written several times in a row. So count every word. Take all the 6-letter words of the exon (letters 1–6, 2–7, 3–8 and so on: 323 of them), put identical ones together and count them. Build it one step at a time – run each line and look at what it gives before you add the next pipe:

awk '{for (i = 1; i <= length($0) - 5; i++) print substr($0, i, 6)}' results/exon19.txt | head
awk '{for (i = 1; i <= length($0) - 5; i++) print substr($0, i, 6)}' results/exon19.txt | sort | uniq -c | head
awk '{for (i = 1; i <= length($0) - 5; i++) print substr($0, i, 6)}' results/exon19.txt | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -12
  • awk runs a small program on each line: here a loop, for (…), that prints the 6 letters starting at each position, substr($0, i, 6) ($0 is the whole line).
  • sort puts identical words next to each other; uniq -c counts each run of identical lines; sort -rn sorts by that count (-n: as numbers, -r: largest first).
Check

CCATCC comes 9 times and TCCCAT 6 times; five words come 5 times each, among them CTGTCC, TGTCCA and CCTCCC.

Think

There are 46 = 4,096 different 6-letter words. In 323 random positions, how many times would you expect to see one particular word? So is a word that comes 5 times likely to be chance?

3. Where they are, and how far apart

grep -o prints each match of a pattern, and -b the position where it starts (counting from 0, like a BED file):

grep -ob CTGTCC results/exon19.txt
Your turn

What are the distances between the positions? Do the same for TGTCCA and CCTCCC: are they the same distances? What does that tell you about the length of the repeated unit, and how many units there are? Write it in your logbook.

Show the answer

CTGTCC starts at 54, 108, 162, 216 and 270: every 54 letters. The other words are 54 apart as well, so the unit is 54 letters long, and there are five units, from position 54 to 323 (in the exon's own numbering from 0). 54 letters are 18 codons.

Now cut out the units and print them one under another: cut -c 55-324 keeps characters 55–324 (counting from 1 this time), and fold -w 54 breaks them into lines of 54. nl numbers the lines.

cut -c 55-324 results/exon19.txt | fold -w 54 | nl

The same with a regular expression, a pattern: . means any one letter, and \{48\} "48 of the thing before". So CTGTCC.\{48\} is CTGTCC and the next 48 letters – one unit. Save the units in a file; you will use them again.

grep -o 'CTGTCC.\{48\}' results/exon19.txt > results/units_GRCh38.txt
cat results/units_GRCh38.txt

Your turn: sites for kinases

Kinases put phosphate groups on serines (S) and threonines (T) – and the speed of the clock depends on how fast PER proteins are phosphorylated. How many S and T does each protein unit have? awk '{print $0, gsub(/[ST]/, "&")}' prints each line with the number of S and T in it (gsub replaces each match with itself, and says how many there were). What fraction of the repeat is S or T? How many sites does the five-repeat allele have that a four-repeat allele lacks?

Show the answer 
cut -c 972-1061 results/PER3_protein.txt | fold -w 18 | awk '{print $0, gsub(/[ST]/, "&")}'

6, 6, 7, 6 and 8: 33 of the 90 amino acids (37%) are S or T – about one in three. One more unit adds 6–7 possible phosphorylation sites (which unit is "the extra one" is a question for stage 5).

Think
  • The exon is 328 letters, but the units cover 270 of them (5 × 54). Is 54 a multiple of 3? Why does that matter for a repeat inside a gene? What would happen to the protein after the repeat if a unit were 53 or 55 letters long?
  • The reference genome is one person's DNA here – and this person had five units on this chromosome. Others have four. What other differences could there be between people, apart from the number of units?
