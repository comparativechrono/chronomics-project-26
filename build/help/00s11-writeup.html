Stage 11 · Write it up

Weeks 11–12as long as it takes

You have results nobody had before you. Now make them into a report that someone else can read, check and repeat: a story with figures, methods precise enough to follow, and an analysis that runs again from start to finish with one command.

You will learn
  • to turn notebooks and commands into scripts that rebuild every result
  • to record the versions of programs and data, and cite them
  • to plan figures, and write methods, results and discussion
First

Ask your supervisor what the report must look like: its length, its sections, the style of references, the deadline – and whether a poster or a talk goes with it. Then plan backwards from the deadline.

1. The story and the figures

Before you write sentences, decide what the reader should learn, in order, and which figure shows each step. A typical set:

FigureShowsFrom
1The gene, the exon and the repeat; the units of the four kinds of allele, and their proteinsstages 2 and 5 (draw it, in R or by hand)
2The frequency of the five-repeat allele by continental group (HPRC) and in 26 populations (1000 Genomes)stages 6 and 8
3r² between the repeat and the SNVs around it; the tagstage 7
4The regional plot of chronotype, coloured by r² with the repeat; the effects of the tag on sleep traitsstage 9
5The number of units on the primate treestage 10

Each figure needs a title, labelled axes with units, a legend, and a caption that says what is shown, how many people or haplotypes, and what the reader should notice. Tables are better than figures for a few exact numbers (the concordance, the PheWAS).

2. Methods: what, with what, in what order

Write the methods so that someone else could repeat your analysis. Name every data set (with its version or release, and the build of the genome: GRCh38 or GRCh37), every program (with its version) and every step. Get the versions:

samtools --version | head -1
bcftools --version | head -1
seqtk 2>&1 | grep -i version
kalign --version 2>&1 | head -3
FastTree 2>&1 | head -1
Rscript -e 'R.version.string'

Cite the data and the programs: The project data lists the papers for the data, and Programs and versions for the programs. If you used the AI assistant, say what for.

3. Make it repeatable

Your results came from commands typed in a terminal and cells run in notebooks, over weeks. Could you get exactly the same numbers again? The way to be sure is to put every step in scripts and run them, from nothing, in one go.

  1. For each stage, copy the commands that made your results (from your logbook, or history in the terminal) into a shell script in ~/project/scripts – for example stage3_haplotypes.sh. Only the commands that matter, in order, with a comment above each.
  2. Turn each notebook into an R script: in the Files tab, make a new file (the + button) such as ~/project/scripts/stage5_structure.R, and copy the notebook's code cells into it, in order. (The notebook's Download button also gives you its code as an R script, on your computer.) Every script must read its inputs from files and write its outputs to files – nothing may depend on what happened to be in memory. Save figures with png() and dev.off(). Test each script on its own in the terminal: Rscript scripts/stage5_structure.R.
  3. Write one script that runs all the others, in order:
#!/bin/bash
# run_all.sh – rebuild every result of the PER3 VNTR project from the data in /data/examples/per3
set -e                        # stop at the first error
cd ~/project
bash scripts/stage2_repeat.sh
bash scripts/stage3_haplotypes.sh
bash scripts/stage4_counts.sh
Rscript scripts/stage5_structure.R
Rscript scripts/stage6_populations.R
bash scripts/stage7_snv_table.sh
Rscript scripts/stage7_ld.R
bash scripts/stage8_1000g.sh
Rscript scripts/stage8_1000g.R
Rscript scripts/stage9_gwas.R
Rscript scripts/stage10_primates.R
  1. Test it: move your results away (mv results results_old; mv figures figures_old; mkdir results figures), run bash scripts/run_all.sh, and compare the new results with the old (diff results/vntr_alleles.tsv results_old/vntr_alleles.tsv says nothing if they are identical). Fix whatever breaks – there will be something – until it runs from start to end.
  2. Write ~/project/README.md: what the project is, what is in each folder, and how to run run_all.sh.
Why bother?

Because you will find a mistake in stage 4 the week before the deadline, and with run_all.sh you fix one line and everything after it is right again – the figures too. And because a result that cannot be repeated is not yet a result.

4. Results: numbers with their uncertainty

Report what you found, in the order of the figures, with the numbers that matter and their uncertainty: frequencies with confidence intervals, r² with the number of haplotypes, effects with their 95% confidence intervals and P values. Keep interpretation for the discussion. A checklist of the results you have:

  • The alleles of the repeat: how many units, how many haplotypes, the four kinds and what distinguishes them; the changes to the protein.
  • Their frequencies, by group and population, in the HPRC and in the 1000 Genomes Project; Hardy–Weinberg.
  • The tag SNPs, their r² overall and by group, the concordance in 222 people, and the exception.
  • What short reads saw of the repeat.
  • The tag in the GWAS of chronotype and sleep; the confidence interval and power; the real signal at PER3; gene expression.
  • The repeat in the primates.

5. Discussion

Questions to answer in your own words – there are no answers here, and your supervisor will want to discuss them with you:

  • What do your results say about the claims of the small studies (chronotype, sleep homeostasis, delayed sleep phase)? What can they not say? (What was measured in UK Biobank, and in whom? What did Viola et al. measure?)
  • The four-repeat alleles are not all alike. What does the 4B branch mean for studies of "4/4 versus 5/5"?
  • How much would you trust the pangenome's sequences of a repeat? The tag? The approximation of section 4 of stage 9?
  • What did the primates tell you about the repeat, and about how important its exact length is likely to be?
  • What would you do next, with more time, data or money? (Individual data of UK Biobank; long-read genomes of people with known chronotype; the repeat in mice …)

6. Keep it

  • My files → Download all my files gives you a zip of everything: your project folder, notebooks and logbook. Keep it somewhere safe – and keep the logbook with it.
  • If your supervisor agrees, put the scripts, the README and the figures in a repository on GitHub: it is the usual way to share an analysis, and a good thing to show when you apply for your next position.
