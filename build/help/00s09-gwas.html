GWAS in two paragraphs

A genome-wide association study tests millions of SNPs, one at a time, for a difference in a trait between people with different genotypes. For each SNP it reports the effect allele, the effect – how much the trait changes per copy of that allele (BETA; for a yes/no trait a log odds ratio, LOGOR) – its standard error (SE), and the P value. Because millions of tests are done, a SNP counts as associated only if P < 5 × 10−8, "genome-wide significance". The full results of a study are its summary statistics; many are public.

UK Biobank recruited half a million people aged 40–69 in Britain in 2006–2010 and asked them, among many things, "Do you consider yourself to be definitely a 'morning' person, more a 'morning' than 'evening' person, more an 'evening' than a 'morning' person, definitely an 'evening' person?" Jones et al. (2019) turned the answers into a chronotype score in which higher means more of a morning person (read their methods for exactly how), and tested 449,734 people of European ancestry. You have their results for the 600,000 letters around PER3, and those of four other studies of sleep (The project data).

Positions change between builds; rsIDs do not. Join data by rsID, or convert positions with a liftover tool, and always write down which build a position is in.

Which allele?

rs228701 is a C/G SNP. C and G pair with each other, so the same SNP read from the other strand of the DNA is G/C – and nothing in the alleles says which strand a study used. (A/T SNPs have the same trap.) Here the studies give alleles on the + strand of the reference, as the pangenome and 1000 Genomes do, and the allele frequency agrees: the effect allele, C, has a frequency of 0.34 in UK Biobank and the C allele (five repeats) 0.335 in the Europeans of the 1000 Genomes Project. Check both whenever you join a GWAS to other data.

Check

"No significant effect" is not the same as "no effect". The 95% confidence interval says which effects the data are compatible with:

ci <- tag$BETA + c(-1.96, 1.96) * tag$SE
ci

And the study's power: the smallest effect it would have found 80% of the time is about (z for the significance level + z for 80% power) × SE.

(qnorm(1 - 0.05 / 2) + qnorm(0.8)) * tag$SE      # at P < 0.05
(qnorm(1 - 5e-8 / 2) + qnorm(0.8)) * tag$SE      # at P < 5e-8
Check

The confidence interval runs from −0.0085 to +0.0026. With 80% power the study would have detected an effect of 0.008 at P < 0.05, and of 0.018 at genome-wide significance.

The T allele of rs61773390 (coded 1) is found only on 4B haplotypes – on 58 of the 85 – and on one rare four-repeat allele: never on 4A, 5A or 5B. The strongest chronotype signal at PER3 marks a branch of the four-repeat alleles, not the number of units. Its T allele makes people more of a morning person (+0.034 per copy), and so does the C allele of rs2640909, the 4B missense change of stage 5 (P = 1.3 × 10−4). Because some four-repeat alleles carry this "morning" branch, the four-repeat allele as a whole looks slightly more "morning" than the five-repeat – the opposite of what Archer et al. reported.

What this means

A GWAS signal points to a haplotype, not to a variant: the cause could be any variant on the haplotypes that carry rs61773390's T allele – or something else entirely, on the same branch. What the data say clearly is that the repeat length itself does not carry the chronotype association of PER3. Whether it has a small effect of its own, once the 4B branch is accounted for, needs a conditional analysis of the individual data – a question for your discussion.

4. Other sleep traits

Does the tag show anything for the other traits? The files name their columns differently, so write a function that finds the right columns in each:

look <- function(file, snp = "rs228701") {
  x <- read.delim(paste0(gw, file))
  id <- intersect(c("SNP", "variant_id"), names(x))[1]
  r <- x[which(x[[id]] == snp), ]                  # which(), again, to skip the rows without an ID
  col <- function(pattern) grep(pattern, names(r), value = TRUE, ignore.case = TRUE)[1]
  data.frame(effect_allele = r[[col("^(ALLELE1|effect_allele)$")]],
             effect = r[[col("^(BETA|LOGOR)")]],
             se = r[[col("^(SE|LOGOR_SE|standard_error)")]],
             p = r[[col("^P")]])
}

6. Does the repeat change how much PER3 is made?

GTEx measured the RNA of every gene in 49 tissues of about a thousand people who had died, 13 of the tissues from the brain, and genotyped them. A variant associated with the amount of a gene's RNA is an eQTL; GTEx reports its normalised effect size (NES) for the alternative allele – for rs228701 that is G, the four-repeat tag. You can ask GTEx's API yourself:

Going further: is there an effect of the repeat once the 4B branch is taken into account?

The four-repeat alleles include the 4B branch, which carries the "morning" allele of rs61773390. So comparing four- with five-repeat alleles mixes two things. With individual data you would fit both variants in one model. With summary statistics there is an approximation: if z1 and z2 are the z-scores (BETA / SE) of two SNPs and r the correlation between them (with their effect alleles counted), the z-score of SNP 1 adjusted for SNP 2 is about (z1 − r z2) / √(1 − r2). You need r in people like those of UK Biobank: the 503 unrelated Europeans of the 1000 Genomes Project.

bcftools query -i 'POS==7829744 || POS==7824465' -f '%POS\t%REF\t%ALT[\t%GT]\n' /data/examples/per3/1000g/1kgp_per3_gene.vcf.gz > results/1kgp_tag_and_peak.tsv
bcftools query -l /data/examples/per3/1000g/1kgp_per3_gene.vcf.gz > results/1kgp_samples_in_vcf_order.txt

ids <- readLines("~/project/results/1kgp_samples_in_vcf_order.txt")
info <- read.delim("/data/examples/per3/1000g/1kgp_samples.tsv")
eur <- ids %in% info$sample[info$superpopulation == "EUR" & info$unrelated_2504 == "yes"]
alt_copies <- function(row) sapply(strsplit(unlist(x[row, -(1:3)]), "|", fixed = TRUE), function(a) sum(as.integer(a)))
c_tag <- 2 - alt_copies(which(x$V1 == 7829744))    # copies of C, the effect allele of rs228701 (REF)
g_peak <- 2 - alt_copies(which(x$V1 == 7824465))   # copies of G, the effect allele of rs61773390 (REF)
r <- cor(c_tag[eur], g_peak[eur])
r
conditional <- function(z1, z2, r) { z <- (z1 - r * z2) / sqrt(1 - r^2); c(z_adjusted = z, p = 2 * pnorm(-abs(z))) }
peak <- chrono[chrono$SNP == "rs61773390", ]
conditional(tag$BETA / tag$SE, peak$BETA / peak$SE, r)

Show what to expect

r = 0.334. For chronotype, the tag's z goes from −1.05 to about +2.5 (P ≈ 0.014): once the 4B branch is accounted for, the five-repeat allele leans towards morning. For the sleep midpoint, it goes from +3.35 to about +2.0 (P ≈ 0.05): still towards later sleep. The two adjusted results point in opposite directions, and both move a lot when r moves a little, because the peak's z is so large. The honest conclusion is that summary statistics cannot settle whether the repeat has a small effect of its own – a question for an analysis of the individual data of UK Biobank, and a good paragraph for your discussion.
