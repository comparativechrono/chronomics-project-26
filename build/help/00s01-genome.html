Stage 1 · The terminal and the genome

Weeks 1–2about 5 hours

Before you can study a repeat in hundreds of genomes, you need to be able to find your way around one. In this stage you learn to give the computer instructions by typing them, and use them to find PER3 in the human genome: where it is, how it is built, what lies around it – and what it looks like in a genome browser.

You will learn
  • the terminal: commands, options and arguments; files, folders and paths
  • to look into big files without opening them: head, grep, cut, sort, uniq, wc, awk – and to join them with pipes, |
  • the three file formats of a genome: FASTA (the sequence), GFF3 and BED (where the genes are) – and their coordinates
  • samtools faidx, to cut any stretch out of a genome
  • a genome browser, and a database you ask from the terminal

1. Your first commands

The Terminal tab on the right is a Linux terminal, the way most bioinformatics is done. The line that ends in $ is the prompt: the computer is waiting for a command. Type one and press Enter. Type these four yourself – one at a time – rather than using the button:

pwd
ls /data/examples/per3
ls -l /data/examples/per3/reference
ls -lh /data/examples/per3/reference
  • pwd – print working directory: the folder you are in. It is your home folder, /home/user, which can also be written ~.
  • ls – list the files in a folder. /data/examples/per3 is the folder of the project's data (you can read it but not change it).
  • -l is an option: it makes ls show a long list, with the size and date of each file. -lh shows the sizes in human units (K, M). Options change what a command does; arguments (here the folder) tell it what to work on.

Three things save a lot of typing: Tab completes the name of a file or a command (type ls /data/ex and press Tab); ↑ brings back the commands you typed before; and man ls (or ls --help) explains a command and all its options. help lists the programs of this terminal.

Your project folder

Keep everything you make in one place. Make a folder, project, with three folders in it – for results, figures and scripts – and go into it:

mkdir -p ~/project/results ~/project/figures ~/project/scripts
cd ~/project
ls
pwd

mkdir makes folders (-p: with any folders above them, and no complaint if they exist already); cd – change directory – moves you into one. A path says where a file is: /data/examples/per3/hprc starts at the top of the file system (an absolute path); results/exon19.fa starts from where you are (a relative path); ~ is your home; .. is the folder above.

pwd prints /home/user/project, and ls shows figures, results and scripts. The Files tab shows the same folders: it is another view of the same files.

2. A genome is a text file

A genome sequence is kept in a FASTA file: a line that starts with > and names the sequence, then the sequence itself, a few dozen letters to a line. The project has the first 8.1 million letters of chromosome 1 of the human reference genome, GRCh38 – the stretch with PER3 in it. The file is compressed (.gz); zcat uncompresses it on the fly, and the pipe, |, sends its output to head, which keeps the first lines:

zcat /data/examples/per3/reference/GRCh38_chr1_8Mb.fa.gz | head -3

Ns stand for letters that are not known. The very tip of the chromosome, its telomere, is a long run of repeats that GRCh38 (released in 2013) leaves as a gap of Ns, as it does for a few other stretches that could not be pieced together; the first complete, telomere-to-telomere human genome, CHM13, filled them in 2022. To see real DNA, cut out a piece from the middle with samtools faidx – sam tools, fasta index. Positions are written chromosome:start-end, counting from 1:

samtools faidx /data/examples/per3/reference/GRCh38_chr1_8Mb.fa.gz chr1:7829834-7830161

This works without reading the whole file because the genome has an index, the .fai file next to it. Look at it:

cat /data/examples/per3/reference/GRCh38_chr1_8Mb.fa.gz.fai

Its five columns: the name of the sequence, its length, where in the file the sequence starts, the letters per line and the bytes per line (one more, for the end of the line). With these, a program can jump straight to any position.

seqtk comp counts the letters of every sequence in a FASTA file: its columns are the name, the length, and the numbers of A, C, G and T (then other counts; man seqtk lists them all):

seqtk comp /data/examples/per3/reference/GRCh38_chr1_8Mb.fa.gz
Check

The length is 8,100,000, of which 1,922,830 are A, 2,039,186 C, 2,028,773 G and 1,905,702 T. The rest – 203,509 letters – are N.

Your turn

What fraction of the bases that are not N are G or C – the GC content? awk can do the sum: in awk '{print ($4 + $5) / ($3 + $4 + $5 + $6)}', $3 means the third column. Send the output of seqtk comp into it with a pipe. Then do the same for PER3 alone, chr1:7784183-7845181 (samtools faidx into seqtk comp, then awk). Is the gene richer or poorer in GC than the region as a whole?

Show a hint

Three programs joined by two pipes: samtools faidx … chr1:7784183-7845181 | seqtk comp | awk '…'. When you give seqtk no file name, it reads what comes through the pipe.

Show the answer 
seqtk comp /data/examples/per3/reference/GRCh38_chr1_8Mb.fa.gz | awk '{print ($4 + $5) / ($3 + $4 + $5 + $6)}'
samtools faidx /data/examples/per3/reference/GRCh38_chr1_8Mb.fa.gz chr1:7784183-7845181 | seqtk comp | awk '{print ($4 + $5) / ($3 + $4 + $5 + $6)}'

About 0.515 for the region and 0.406 for PER3. The tip of chromosome 1 is unusually rich in GC – the human genome as a whole is about 41% GC – and PER3, most of which is introns, is poorer in GC than its surroundings.

3. Where are the genes?

A sequence says nothing about where the genes are; that is kept in an annotation file. genes_GRCh38.gff3 lists the seven protein-coding genes of chr1:7.4–8.1 Mb, with the exons and coding sequence (CDS) of one transcript each – the MANE Select transcript, the one that the clinical and research databases have agreed to use. Look at the start of it (cut -c 1-150 cuts each line to its first 150 characters, so that the long lines fit):

head -6 /data/examples/per3/reference/genes_GRCh38.gff3 | cut -c 1-150

A GFF3 file has nine columns separated by tabs: the sequence, the source, the type of feature (gene, mRNA, exon, CDS), its start and end (counted from 1, both included), a score, the strand (+ or -: which of the two strands of DNA the gene is read from), the reading frame, and a column of name=value attributes separated by ;.

awk is the tool for columns. This line keeps the rows whose third column is gene and prints the start, end, strand and the second attribute (the name):

awk -F'\t' '$3 == "gene" {split($9, a, ";"); print $4, $5, $7, a[2]}' /data/examples/per3/reference/genes_GRCh38.gff3

-F'\t' says the columns are separated by tabs; $3 == "gene" is the condition; what is between { } is done for each row that meets it.

Check

PER3 runs from 7,784,183 to 7,845,181 on the + strand. Its neighbours are VAMP3 just before it and UTS2 after it.

  2. Look at UTS2's start and end. What do you notice about it and PER3? On which strand is it? (UTS2 makes urotensin II, a peptide hormone that constricts blood vessels.)

Which transcript is it (its ENST number)? How many exons does it have (grep its ENST number, then grep -c -w exon counts the lines with the word exon)?

The exons, and a trap: BED files count from 0

PER3_exons.bed lists the exons of that transcript, one per line, in the BED format: sequence, start, end, name, score, strand.

cat /data/examples/per3/reference/PER3_exons.bed

Find exon 19 in it, and compare its start with the position you gave samtools faidx above (7,829,834). They differ by one. BED files count from 0 and do not include the end: the first base of a chromosome is 0, and a feature from 0 to 10 has the bases 0–9. GFF3, VCF files, genome browsers and samtools count from 1 and include both ends. Both say the same thing; mixing them up – being off by one – is the commonest slip in genomics. A good thing about BED: the length is simply end − start.

awk '{print $4, $3 - $2}' /data/examples/per3/reference/PER3_exons.bed
awk '{s = s + $3 - $2} END {print s}' /data/examples/per3/reference/PER3_exons.bed
Check

PER3's transcript has 22 exons. Exon 19 is 328 bases long (in 1-based positions, 7,829,834–7,830,161). Together the exons have 6,365 bases – about a tenth of the gene; the rest is introns, which are spliced out of the RNA.

Think

328 is not a multiple of 3. How can an exon in the middle of a gene not hold a whole number of codons? (Hint: what happens to the codon that is cut by an intron when the RNA is spliced?)

4. Look at it

The Genome browser tab draws the same files. It opens on the project's piece of GRCh38, with PER3's exons. Go to the whole gene, then to exon 19:

  • Exons are boxes, introns are lines; the little arrows on the lines show the direction in which the gene is read. Find UTS2 at the right: which way do its arrows point?
  • Type a position or a gene name in the box at the top, or drag sideways to move; + and − zoom. Zoom into exon 19 until you can read its letters.
  • You can open it from the terminal too: igv --locus chr1:7,829,834-7,830,161 (Genome browser has more).

5. Ask a database

Everything you have found so far came from files someone gave you. Check it against the source. Ensembl is one of the big databases of genomes and genes; it answers questions over the internet – a REST API – and replies in JSON, a text format of "name": value pairs. curl fetches the reply, and jq picks the parts you want:

curl -s "https://rest.ensembl.org/lookup/symbol/homo_sapiens/PER3?content-type=application/json" | jq '.id, .start, .end, .strand, .description'

Then look at PER3's gene and protein in the Databases tab, where the same databases have forms to fill in:

Check

Ensembl gives the same start, end and strand as the GFF3 file (1 means the + strand), and the gene's Ensembl ID, ENSG00000049246. In UniProt the human PER3 protein is P56645, 1,201 amino acids long.
