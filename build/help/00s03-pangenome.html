Stage 3 · From a pangenome to 462 haplotypes

Week 3about 5 hours

GRCh38 is one chromosome of one person at this spot. The Human Pangenome Reference Consortium has assembled both copies of chromosome 1 – both haplotypes – of 231 people from long reads. In this stage you learn the format their differences are shared in, find the repeat in it, and rebuild the 600 letters around the repeat in each of the 462 chromosomes.

You will learn
  • the VCF format: variants, alleles, genotypes, phasing
  • bcftools, the standard program for VCF files: view, query, consensus
  • the shell as a programming language: variables, loops and $( )
  • a habit: try a command on one case, then on three, then on all
A pangenome

Each of us has two copies of chromosome 1, one from each parent. A reference genome is a single copy; a pangenome holds many. The HPRC sequenced each person with long reads (tens of thousands of letters each, long enough to span a repeat), assembled the two copies of every chromosome separately, and aligned all of them into one graph: where the copies agree, one path; where they differ, a bubble with a branch for each version. Every bubble, written down against GRCh38, becomes a line of a VCF file (Liao et al. 2023; Hickey et al. 2024 – see the reading list). You have the 2,000 letters around the repeat: hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz.

1. A VCF file

A VCF (variant call format) file is a text file with a header – lines starting with ## that say what the file holds, and one starting with #CHROM that names the columns – and then one line for each variant. It is compressed and indexed, and bcftools reads it. Look at the header:

cd ~/project
bcftools view -h /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | head -8
bcftools view -h /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | tail -1 | cut -f 1-12
bcftools query -l /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | wc -l

bcftools query -l lists the people (the samples), one column each after the ninth. Now the variants (view -H: without the header), their first eight columns:

bcftools view -H /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | wc -l
bcftools view -H /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | cut -f 1-8 | head -5
ColumnWhat it holds
CHROM, POSwhere the variant is (counting from 1)
IDits name, such as an rsID – here .: none (you will add them in stage 7)
REF, ALTthe letters of the reference genome there, and the other versions – the alleles – separated by commas
QUAL, FILTERquality and filters (not used here)
INFOfacts about the variant: AC, how many chromosomes carry each ALT allele; AN, how many chromosomes were counted
FORMAT, then one column per personthe genotype (GT) of each person

bcftools query -f prints the parts you name, in your own layout: %POS is the position, \t a tab, \n the end of a line.

bcftools query -f '%POS\t%REF\t%ALT\t%AC\n' /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz
Check

231 people and 26 variants. Most of them change one letter for another (an SNV: single-nucleotide variant); AN=462 – two chromosomes per person.

Your turn: find the repeat

One of the 26 lines is the repeat. Look at the lengths of the alleles: awk 'length($2) > 1' keeps the lines whose second column (REF) is longer than one letter. Then look at that whole line with bcftools view -H -r chr1:POSITION (-r: only this region). How long is each allele? How do the ALT alleles differ from REF, and from each other? Which allele is a four-repeat allele?

Show the answer 
bcftools query -f '%POS\t%REF\t%ALT\t%AC\n' /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | awk 'length($2) > 1'
bcftools view -H -r chr1:7829992 /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | cut -f 1-8

The repeat is the line at 7,829,992. REF is 56 letters of the five-repeat reference. ALT 1 is the same 56 letters with one letter changed (an A to a G): it is still a five-repeat allele, a slightly different one. ALT 2 (CA) and ALT 3 (CG) are 2 letters: 54 letters – one unit – are missing. They are four-repeat alleles. (The other long line, at 7,830,272, deletes one letter.)

Where in a repeat a unit is "missing" cannot really be said: removing any 54 letters in a row from a run of near-identical units gives nearly the same result. The pangenome's graph had to choose one way to write it. That is why repeats are hard to describe – and to call from short reads.

2. Genotypes

A genotype like 2|1 says which allele each of the person's two chromosomes carries: 0 is REF, 1 the first ALT allele, 2 the second, and so on. The bar | means the genotype is phased: we know that the first allele is on one chromosome (haplotype 1) and the second on the other (haplotype 2). (A / would mean we only know the two alleles, not which chromosome each is on. Long reads and assembly make phasing easy.)

bcftools query -r chr1:7829992 -f '[%SAMPLE %GT\n]' /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | head
bcftools query -r chr1:7829992 -f '[%GT\n]' /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | sort | uniq -c
Your turn

Count the alleles rather than the genotypes: break each genotype in two (tr '|' '\n' turns each bar into the end of a line) before you sort and count. How many of the 462 chromosomes carry four units, and how many five?

Show the answer 
bcftools query -r chr1:7829992 -f '[%GT\n]' /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz | tr '|' '\n' | sort | uniq -c

26 chromosomes carry REF (0) and 109 ALT 1 (1): 135 five-repeat alleles. 326 carry ALT 2 and one carries ALT 3: 327 four-repeat alleles. Compare the numbers with AC on the line.

HG00099 is 2|1: haplotype 1 has four units and haplotype 2 five. In all, 327 four-repeat and 135 five-repeat chromosomes.

3. Rebuild one haplotype

You could stop here: you have the number of units in every chromosome. But the VCF writes the repeat in a way chosen by a graph program, and it hides what the units of each allele look like. To study them you need the sequence itself. A VCF lists the differences from the reference; put a haplotype's differences into the reference and you have its sequence back. That is what bcftools consensus does. First, the 600 letters of GRCh38 around the repeat:

samtools faidx /data/examples/per3/reference/GRCh38_chr1_8Mb.fa.gz chr1:7829701-7830300 > results/ref_region.fa
cat results/ref_region.fa

Then the two haplotypes of HG00099 – -s names the person, -H the haplotype (1 or 2):

bcftools consensus -f results/ref_region.fa -s HG00099 -H 1 /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz > results/HG00099_hap1.fa
bcftools consensus -f results/ref_region.fa -s HG00099 -H 2 /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz > results/HG00099_hap2.fa
cat results/HG00099_hap1.fa results/HG00099_hap2.fa | seqtk comp | cut -f 1,2
grep -v ">" results/HG00099_hap1.fa | tr -d "\n" | grep -o CTGTCC | wc -l
Check

Haplotype 1 is 546 letters long with 4 copies of CTGTCC; haplotype 2 is 600 letters with 5. 600 − 546 = 54: one unit.

4. All 462: a loop

You could type that command 462 times. Instead, a loop does it for you. A loop repeats some commands once for each item of a list; each time, a variable holds the item, and $ before its name gives its value. Try a small one first:

bcftools query -l /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz > results/hprc_people.txt
head -3 results/hprc_people.txt
for s in $(head -3 results/hprc_people.txt); do
  echo "person $s"
done

$( … ) runs a command and puts what it prints in its place – here, the list of the first three people. Now two loops, one inside the other: for each person, for each of the two haplotypes, build the sequence and add it to one file. sed renames each sequence (the > line) to the person and haplotype, >> adds it to the end of haplotypes.fa, and 2>/dev/null throws away the messages bcftools writes as it works. Run it on three people first:

rm -f results/haplotypes.fa
for s in $(head -3 results/hprc_people.txt); do
  for h in 1 2; do
    bcftools consensus -f results/ref_region.fa -s $s -H $h /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz 2>/dev/null | sed "s/^>.*/>${s}_hap${h}/" >> results/haplotypes.fa
  done
done
grep ">" results/haplotypes.fa

Six sequences, named HG00097_hap1 to HG00126_hap2? Then run it on everyone (cat in place of head -3). It takes a minute or two – watch the files grow in the Files tab.

rm -f results/haplotypes.fa
for s in $(cat results/hprc_people.txt); do
  for h in 1 2; do
    bcftools consensus -f results/ref_region.fa -s $s -H $h /data/examples/per3/hprc/hprc_per3_vntr.vcf.gz 2>/dev/null | sed "s/^>.*/>${s}_hap${h}/" >> results/haplotypes.fa
  done
done
grep -c ">" results/haplotypes.fa
seqtk comp results/haplotypes.fa | cut -f 2 | sort | uniq -c
Check

462 sequences: 327 of 546 letters, 132 of 600 and 3 of 599.

Think
  • Why does phasing matter here? What could you not do if the genotypes were written 2/1?
  • You rebuilt 600 letters, not a whole chromosome. Why is that enough for this question? What would you miss if a variant between two units had been left out of the VCF?
  • The VCF was made by comparing each chromosome with GRCh38. What happens to a sequence that GRCh38 does not have at all – an insertion longer than anything in the reference?
